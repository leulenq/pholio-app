# Legal Research: AI Semantic Search Over Talent Photos & Bios (Pholio)

**Prepared:** September 2, 2026. **Scope:** Legal risk analysis for (A) text embeddings of talent bios/self-declared stats, and (B) image embeddings / VLM captions of talent's own photographs, used to power agency-facing semantic casting search, including "find talent whose photos resemble this reference image."

**How to read this:** Law here is young and, in several places, actively unsettled or in flux as of the September 2026 cutoff. Every section flags confidence level. Cite-checked against primary sources and 2025–2026 secondary sources; anything older than 2024 is flagged.

## 1. Biometric privacy statutes (Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI/TRAIGA, Washington HB 1493/My Health My Data, Colorado AI Act)

### 1.1 Is a whole-image CLIP/SigLIP embedding a "biometric identifier" / "scan of face geometry"?

**Unsettled — genuine litigation risk, not merely theoretical.**

- BIPA (740 ILCS 14) defines "biometric identifier" as "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry." A "scan of face geometry" is understood as the mathematical map built by measuring facial landmarks into a compact numeric template — a *faceprint* ([Morrison Foerster, "Getting BIPA Right," May 2024](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240503-getting-bipa-right-biometric-identifiers-must-identify)).
- A CLIP/SigLIP embedding of a whole photograph is not built the same way (it is not a purpose-built facial landmark map; it encodes the entire scene — clothing, lighting, background — not solely facial geometry). But it is a numeric representation derived in part from the face and is technically capable of supporting face-matching if repurposed for that task. Academic literature explicitly raises this: CLIP-family embeddings *can* be used for facial recognition and *can* leak identity even when not trained for it ("Face Recognition in the age of CLIP & Billion-image datasets," [arXiv:2301.07315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.07315); "From Measurement to Mitigation… Identity Leakage in Image Representation Encoders," [arXiv:2604.05296](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05296)). No Illinois appellate or Illinois Supreme Court decision has yet ruled squarely on whether a general-purpose, non-identification image embedding is a "scan of face geometry." This is genuinely open.
- **The "must it be used to identify" question is itself split among Illinois federal district courts** ([Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240503-getting-bipa-right-biometric-identifiers-must-identify)):
  - *Requiring identifiability:* *Daichendt v. CVS* (capacity to identify is "the most foundational aspect of a BIPA claim"); *Zellmer v. Meta* (BIPA shouldn't reach "individuals unknown" to the company).
  - *Rejecting the requirement:* *Colombo v. YouTube* (claim survived despite no reliable identification method, on blurred video); *Gunderson v. Amazon* (non-user voice claims allowed; court rejected a "minimum known contact" gloss).
  - Multiple additional suits (doorbell cameras, photo-grouping features, Snapchat filters, exam-proctoring eye-tracking, "smart advertising screens") have proceeded on the theory that a biometric identifier can exist under BIPA's text **even where the defendant never identified the plaintiff** — courts note BIPA's definition doesn't expressly require identification-in-fact.
  - **Bottom line for Pholio:** you cannot rely on "we never identify anyone from the vector" as a safe-harbor defense in Illinois; several courts have rejected exactly that argument. The stronger defenses are (a) the embedding is not "a scan of face geometry" because it doesn't isolate/measure facial landmarks, and (b) even if scrutinized, users gave informed written consent (see 1.4).

- **Clearview AI and Facebook/Patel litigation** confirm the extreme end of exposure when face-*geometry* templates are built and used for identification/matching:
  - *Patel v. Facebook* ($550M settlement, 2020) — Facebook's Tag Suggestions built explicit geometric face templates (distances between eyes/nose/ears) used to identify people; this is the paradigm BIPA case and squarely distinguishable from a stylistic image embedding not used to identify anyone across images ([Columbia GFoE case summary](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/patel-v-facebook/); [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/08/victory-lawsuit-may-proceed-against-facebooks-biometric-surveillance-0)).
  - *In re Clearview AI Consumer Privacy Litigation* — a first-of-its-kind $51.75M-value settlement (23% equity stake) approved March 20, 2025, over Clearview's identification-oriented facial-recognition database scraped from 60B+ images ([ISBA](https://www.isba.org/dailylegalnews/2025/07/23/afirstinbipalitigationclassmembersreceiveequityinc); [Loevy & Loevy](https://www.loevy.com/big-wins/judge-oks-loevys-innovative-51-75-million-settlement-in-clearview-ai-class-action-lawsuit/)). Notably, a bipartisan coalition of 22 state AGs + DC filed an amicus **opposing** the settlement as inadequate — signaling continued AG appetite for aggressive biometric enforcement.
  - Both cases involve systems whose *purpose* was person-identification/matching across images. Pholio's proposed design (style/aesthetic similarity search, never identity matching) is meaningfully different — but "we built it for a different purpose" hasn't stopped plaintiffs' firms from suing about incidental capability. **Treat this as elevated litigation risk in Illinois specifically, not settled safety.**

### 1.2 2024 BIPA amendment — per-person accrual (SB 2979)

- Signed August 2, 2024. Overturns *Cothron v. White Castle* (2023 IL 128004), which had held each scan/transmission without consent was a separate violation. Now: repeated capture of the *same* biometric identifier from the *same* person via the *same* collection method is **one violation**, not one per instance ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=3bbbb909-d0f1-4816-808c-cf07da660352); [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/data-privacy/illinois-enacts-bipa-amendment-limiting-violation-accrual/)).
- Damages remain $1,000/violation (negligent) or $5,000/violation (intentional/reckless) — now capped per aggrieved person rather than per scan, which meaningfully reduces (but doesn't eliminate) the "bet the company" exposure that made BIPA infamous. Note the 7th Circuit has since held the amended damages remedy **applies retroactively** ([ABA, May 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-may/7th-circuit-holds-bipa-damages-remedy-applies-retroactively/)) — this narrows exposure for pre-2024 conduct too, but statutory damages still scale with unique users, which at Pholio's roster size is real money if BIPA is found to apply at all.

### 1.3 Texas — CUBI and TRAIGA (2025)

- **CUBI** (Bus. & Com. Code Ch. 503A) already required informed consent before "capture" of a biometric identifier for a commercial purpose; enforceable only by the Texas AG (no private right of action), penalties up to $25,000/violation.
- **TRAIGA** (Texas Responsible AI Governance Act), signed June 22, 2025, effective **January 1, 2026**, amends CUBI:
  - Clarifies that an image being publicly available online does **not** by itself constitute consent to capture biometric identifiers from it, *unless the individual themself made it public* ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/texas-passes-unique-artificial-intelligence-law-focused-on-prohibited-practices); [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/texas-enacts-responsible-ai-governance-act/)). This matters for Pholio because your talent upload their own photos to your own platform — this exception is squarely satisfied (self-published to your service), which is a meaningfully stronger footing than a scraped-image system like Clearview's.
  - New exemption: capture/training/storage of biometric identifiers used to **develop, train, evaluate, or offer an AI model/system is exempt from CUBI**, *unless* the system is used or deployed to uniquely identify a specific individual ([FBT Gibbons](https://fbtgibbons.com/new-texas-ai-law-affects-collection-and-use-of-biometric-identifiers/)). This is directly favorable: an embedding model that powers style-similarity search (not identification) plausibly falls inside this new carve-out — but it is a brand-new, un-litigated provision, so treat as "likely favorable, not proven."
  - Also prohibits AI used to "capture biometric data without consent" as a general prohibited practice.

### 1.4 Washington — HB 1493 (2017, not new) and My Health My Data Act (WMHMDA, 2023)

- HB 1493 (RCW 19.375) is a 2017 law (older than your 2024–2026 window; flagged as such) requiring notice + consent before **enrolling** a biometric identifier in a database for a commercial purpose, with AG enforcement, penalties up to $7,500/violation ([FKKS](https://fkks.com/news/third-state-adopts-biometric-privacy-law); [TermsFeed](https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/washington-biometric-privacy-law-hb-1493/)).
- WMHMDA (RCW 19.373, 2023) separately regulates "consumer health data," which includes biometric data "generated from measurement or technological processing of physiological, biological or behavioral characteristics… that identifies a consumer." This statute has a **private right of action** (via WA's Consumer Protection Act) and is aggressively litigated in 2024–2025 against unrelated ad-tech/analytics defendants. If your image embeddings are ever framed as inferring a health-adjacent trait (even indirectly, e.g., pregnancy, disability appearance cues), WMHMDA risk activates. Recommendation: your Section 4/design guardrails already exclude health inferences — keep that airtight in the model prompt/spec and in QA red-teaming.

### 1.5 Colorado AI Act — currently in flux, not what you may have been told

- SB 24-205 ("Colorado AI Act") was slated for Feb 1, 2026, then delayed to June 30, 2026 by SB 25B-004 (Aug 28, 2025) after a special session failed to reach compromise ([Clark Hill](https://www.clarkhill.com/news-events/news/colorados-ai-law-delayed-until-june-2026-what-the-latest-setback-means-for-businesses/)).
- **April 27, 2026:** a federal magistrate blocked enforcement of SB 205 after xAI sued on constitutional grounds, DOJ intervening in support of xAI.
- **May 14, 2026:** Governor Polis signed **SB 26-189**, a substantial replacement, effective **January 1, 2027**, that strips much of the original "high-risk AI" framework and instead focuses on disclosure/transparency for automated decision-making in "consequential decisions" ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/); [Carpe Datum Law](https://www.carpedatumlaw.com/2026/05/colorados-ai-reset-two-weeks-a-white-house-callout-and-a-pivot-away-from-the-eu-model/)).
- **Practical read as of Sept 2026: Colorado's original EU-style "high-risk AI developer/deployer" regime is dead. The live framework (SB 26-189) doesn't take effect until Jan 1, 2027 and is materially narrower.** Do not over-build for the original SB 24-205 text; monitor SB 26-189's final rules through late 2026.

### 1.6 What consent language / retention schedule would satisfy BIPA if it applied

BIPA §15(b) requires, before collection: (1) written notice that biometric data is being collected/stored, (2) the specific purpose and length of term for which it's collected/stored/used, and (3) a written release signed by the subject (or authorized representative). §15(a) requires a publicly available written retention schedule and destruction guidelines (destroy within 3 years of last interaction, or when purpose is satisfied, whichever first).

A BIPA-safe consent + retention design for Pholio:

- **Standalone written consent** (not buried in general ToS) presented before any embedding processing begins, naming: (a) what is processed (photographs you upload, for the sole purpose of powering agency search matching visual style/casting fit — not identification), (b) that no face-geometry template or identity-matching model is created, (c) exact retention period tied to discoverability status, (d) that withdrawal triggers deletion of derived vectors within a stated window (recommend ≤30 days), (e) that the underlying photos are not deleted, only the derived embeddings.
- **Retention/destruction schedule**, published: image/text embeddings retained only while the talent's discoverability flag is ON; purged within a fixed SLA (e.g., 30 days) of (i) opt-out, (ii) account deletion, or (iii) N months of account inactivity — whichever first, mirroring BIPA §15(a)'s "3 years or purpose satisfied, whichever first" structure but tighter, since your purpose (active discoverability) ends much sooner than 3 years.
- Maintain an audit log of consent grant/withdrawal timestamps and purge completion timestamps — this is your best evidence in any BIPA/CUBI dispute.

## 2. EU AI Act

**Confidence: high on statutory text and dates; medium-low on how your specific feature would be classified — no regulator guidance or case law yet addresses "casting search by visual style similarity."**

### 2.1 Article 5(1)(g) — biometric categorisation ban

- Prohibits AI systems that categorize individuals **based on their biometric data** to **infer or deduce** race, political opinion, trade union membership, religious/philosophical belief, sex life, or sexual orientation ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-the-eu-ai-act-understanding-the-prohibition-of-biometric-categorization-for-certain-sensitive-characteristics/)). Enforceable since **February 2, 2025** (prohibitions tier).
- Key qualifier: the ban is triggered by **intended inference of the listed protected attributes**, not by biometric analysis generally. Non-sensitive categorization (hair colour, styling, mood, lighting register) is outside Article 5(1)(g) *by design* — the Commission's own July 2025 prohibited-practices guidelines note a narrow carve-out permitting "labelling or filtering of lawfully acquired biometric data sets... such as sorting images according to hair colour or eye colour" ([EC Guidelines, July 29 2025](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-08/guidelines_on_prohibited_artificial_intelligence_practices_established_by_regulation_eu_20241689_ai_act_english_ied3r5nwo50xggpcfmwckm3nuc_112367-1.PDF); [FPF summary](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-the-eu-ai-act-understanding-the-prohibition-of-biometric-categorization-for-certain-sensitive-characteristics/)) — but the Commission stresses that carve-out is *intentionally narrow* (originally framed for forensic/law-enforcement dataset sorting) and does not bless general production inference systems.
- **Your stated design (never infer race/ethnicity/skin tone/age/health/protected traits from images) is squarely the compliant design under 5(1)(g).** The legal risk here is not the rule but *execution*: if your captioning VLM or embedding model, even unintentionally, encodes or lets a downstream ranking correlate with a protected trait (e.g., "editorial, androgynous, clean beauty" queries systematically under- or over-representing certain skin tones because of biased training data), that drifts into 5(1)(g) exposure even without an intent to infer race — regulators and plaintiffs will look at *effect*, not just prompt wording. Recommend adversarial/statistical testing of query-result distributions across self-declared heritage/skin-tone bands as an ongoing control, not a one-time check.

### 2.2 Article 3(40) "biometric categorisation system" and Annex III point 1

- A "biometric categorisation system" = an AI system assigning people to categories **based on their biometric data**, unless it's a "purely ancillary feature intrinsically linked to another commercial service... for objective technical reasons" (Art. 3(40), Recital 30). Example given by the Commission: filters that categorize facial/body features for e-commerce virtual try-on are ancillary and out of scope.
- Annex III point 1 makes biometric categorisation systems (according to sensitive/protected attributes, via inference) **high-risk**, alongside remote biometric identification.
- **Whether Pholio's photo-embedding search is a "biometric categorisation system" at all is genuinely unclear and un-litigated.** Two readings:
  1. **Narrow/favorable reading:** Since your embeddings/captions never encode or infer sensitive/protected attributes (by design), and the system doesn't "assign persons to categories" on protected axes, it may fall outside Article 3(40) and Annex III point 1 entirely — a style/aesthetic similarity ranking is not "biometric categorisation" in the sense the Act targets (which is about inferring immutable/protected traits), even though it does process images "of" biometric subjects.
  2. **Broad/cautious reading:** A regulator could argue that ranking talent by editorial/beauty "lanes" derived from their photographed physical appearance is itself a categorization *of a person based on biometric data* (the photo), even if the output categories (mood, styling, lighting) are not on the Art. 5(1)(g) protected list — and Annex III point 1 is not textually limited to the same protected-attribute list as Article 5(1)(g) (the Annex III trigger text ties back to "inference of sensitive or protected attributes," so the narrow reading is likely the better one, but this hasn't been tested).
- **No EU regulator or court decision as of Sept 2026 addresses an image-embedding casting/style-matching tool specifically.** This is a live gap; treat as **unsettled**.

### 2.3 Annex III point 4 — employment/recruitment high-risk category

- Covers AI systems used for recruitment/selection (targeted job ads, filtering/analysing applications, evaluating candidates) and for decisions on promotion/termination/task allocation/monitoring of "work-related relationships."
- **Does agency-side casting search for representation count?** This is a genuinely contested classification question with three complicating layers, none of which have EU guidance yet:
  1. **Employment relationship threshold:** Annex III point 4 is keyed to "work-related contractual relationships" and "recruitment." Talent are not employees of the agency; representation is typically an agency contract, and individual bookings are separate (often short-term, sometimes via loan-out or freelance arrangements) engagements with a third-party client (brand/photographer), not with the agency itself. Whether EU regulators would treat "agency ranks talent it represents" as recruitment *of the agency's own workforce* (arguably no — talent aren't being recruited into a job with the searching agency) versus a tool that *facilitates* client-side casting/recruitment decisions (arguably yes, if EU-based brands/casting clients use Pholio search results to make hiring-like selections for a shoot) is unresolved.
  2. **Self-employment coverage:** Annex III point 4 explicitly extends to AI used in "access to self-employment," which is closer to how a talent's initial *signing* by an agency functions — this is the strongest argument for coverage if the search tool is used to shortlist who an agency signs/represents, as opposed to who is booked once represented.
  3. **Practical exposure driver:** the highest-risk use case is if EU-established agencies, or agencies searching for EU-resident talent, use Pholio's tool to shortlist candidates for representation or for a casting decision that functions like a hiring decision for that engagement.
- **Recommendation:** treat any EU-facing (EU-established agency user, or ranking of EU-resident/EU-domiciled talent) deployment of the casting-search feature as a probable Annex III point 4 high-risk system for compliance-planning purposes, even though the exact classification is arguable, because (a) the downside of being wrong is Article 99 penalties up to €15M/3% global turnover, (b) the EU Commission's own August 2026 high-risk employment guidelines are still in draft/consultation as of mid-2026 (comment period closed July 23, 2026; final guidance expected "by the end of 2026" — i.e., **after** the August 2, 2026 compliance deadline for Annex III, illustrating how unsettled this space still is — [DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/eu-commission-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-high-risk-ai-in-employment)).

### 2.4 Timeline — what applies as of August 2026

- Prohibitions (incl. Art. 5(1)(g)): in force since **Feb 2, 2025**. Already binding.
- Full high-risk-system obligations (technical documentation, conformity assessment, CE marking, EU database registration, quality/risk management systems) for **Annex III (Art. 6(2)) stand-alone high-risk systems** — the category recruitment/employment tools fall under — **apply from August 2, 2026**, per most 2025–2026 secondary sources ([SIG](https://www.softwareimprovementgroup.com/blog/eu-ai-act-summary/); [ADVISORI](https://www.advisori.de/en/blog/eu-ai-act-high-risk-what-companies-must-implement-by-august-2026)). **Caveat:** one 2026 source (Holland & Knight, citing recent Commission simplification proposals) frames obligations for Annex III/Article 6(2) systems as possibly deferred further, to **December 2, 2027** — there is a live "digital omnibus" simplification package moving through the EU institutions in 2026 that would push these dates. **Treat the exact date as moving; as of Sept 2026 the safest planning assumption is "high-risk Annex III obligations are either already live (Aug 2026) or landing within roughly 16 months (Dec 2027) — build toward compliance now rather than waiting for certainty.**
- Article 50 transparency obligations (AI-content labeling, disclosure that a person is interacting with AI, deepfake/synthetic-content labeling) — already largely applicable; providers/deployers can self-determine "adequate measures" pending final Commission guidelines.
- Article 27 FRIA (fundamental rights impact assessment) is required of **deployers who are public bodies or private operators providing public services**, plus (per Art. 27(1)) deployers of Annex III high-risk systems used for **credit scoring or life/health insurance risk assessment** — it is *not* a universal high-risk obligation for private employment-adjacent tools by default text, though many high-risk deployers voluntarily perform an equivalent assessment as good practice and some guidance treats FRIA-equivalent analysis as expected for Annex III point 4 systems generally. Recommend performing one anyway, given the classification ambiguity in §2.3.

## 3. NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT bias audit)

**Confidence: medium.** No regulator guidance or enforcement action addresses talent-representation platforms specifically.

- LL144 covers "Automated Employment Decision Tools" — tools using ML/statistical modeling/AI that **substantially assist or replace** discretionary decision-making for **screening candidates for hire, or employees for promotion**, used by an **employer or employment agency**. Threshold trigger is when the AEDT's output is used to "substantially assist" a human decision (not merely be one input among many with meaningful human weighting of other factors) ([Warden AI](https://www.warden-ai.com/resources/automated-employment-decision-tools); [Pivot Point Security](https://www.pivotpointsecurity.com/what-is-nycs-ai-bias-law-and-how-does-it-impact-firms-using-hr-automation/)).
- **The employment-relationship threshold is the crux issue for Pholio, same as under the EU AI Act analysis above.** Talent are generally not employees of the agency using the search tool, and the agency isn't "hiring" them into a job with itself when it books them for a client shoot. LL144's statutory text and DCWP rules are calibrated to *employer-employee* and *employer-independent hiring* dynamics (résumé screening, promotion). Two scenarios diverge:
  1. **Agency uses semantic search to shortlist talent it is deciding whether to *sign/represent*.** This is the closer analogy to "screening candidates" — arguably functionally similar to hiring, even though the legal relationship (agency representation contract) differs from employment. No case law says LL144's "employer/employment agency" language reaches a *talent agency* screening who to represent (talent agencies are not typically "employment agencies" under NY Labor Law Article 11, which has its own separate licensing regime for agencies that procure engagements for a fee — though the overlap between "employment agency" definitions across statutes is not harmonized).
  2. **Client/casting-side users search for talent to book for a specific job/shoot.** This is closer to a casting decision than an employment decision by NYC's usual AEDT paradigm (job screening for a role at *that* employer), and most casting engagements are independent-contractor/loan-out bookings, not employment. This scenario is the weaker case for LL144 coverage but not risk-free, since "employment agency" is defined broadly in some NY contexts and DCWP has signaled expansive intent.
- **Practical read:** LL144 coverage of a talent-representation semantic search tool is **not clearly established either way**; DCWP has not opined on modeling/casting platforms. Given (a) the NY State Comptroller's December 2025 audit finding DCWP enforcement "ineffective" and DCWP's public commitment to more rigorous, proactive enforcement in 2026 ([OSC audit](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/01/critical-audit-of-nyc-ai-hiring-law-signals-increased-risk-for-employers)), and (b) Pholio is NY-headquartered/NY-first, prudence favors treating the "agency shortlists talent to sign" use case as an AEDT-adjacent risk and building toward bias-audit-readiness (documented methodology, ability to run an impact ratio analysis by sex/race/ethnicity category on shortlisting outcomes) even before a formal legal determination that LL144 applies. This is materially cheaper to build in now than retrofit under enforcement pressure.
- Penalties: $500 first violation, up to $1,500/day continuing violation — compounding daily exposure if a tool is later deemed a non-compliant AEDT.

## 4. US privacy law (FTC, CCPA/CPRA, NY Fashion Workers Act, NY synthetic performer law)

### 4.1 FTC — Section 5 unfairness/deception re: facial recognition/image analysis

- **FTC v. Rite Aid (Dec 19, 2023):** banned Rite Aid from using facial recognition for surveillance for 5 years; ordered deletion of images/videos and **all models or algorithms derived from them**; required a documented risk-assessment and bias-testing program before any future biometric deployment ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/12/rite-aid-banned-using-ai-facial-recognition-after-ftc-says-retailer-deployed-technology-without)). Rite Aid's core failure was deploying an *identification/surveillance* system without accuracy testing or bias mitigation — not "image analysis" generally. This is the template the FTC will apply to any biometric-adjacent AI: test for accuracy, test for demographic disparate impact, document safeguards, don't overclaim.
- **IntelliVision (Jan 8, 2025):** consent order barring unsubstantiated claims about facial recognition accuracy/bias — a "say what you do, do what you say" enforcement, not a ban on the technology itself ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2025/2/ftc-sends-a-reminder-to-facial-recognition-tech-companies-to-do-what-they-say-and-say-what-they-do)).
- FTC's 2023 biometric policy statement (still current) puts companies on notice that *unfair or deceptive* practices around biometric data include: false claims about accuracy/bias, failure to test/monitor for disparate demographic performance, using biometric data beyond disclosed purpose, inadequate safeguards.
- **Applicability to Pholio:** your model (never infer protected attributes, purpose-limited processing, opt-in, deletion on withdrawal) is squarely inside the safe-harbor direction the FTC is signaling. The exposure would come from (a) mismatched consent language vs. actual data use (a classic FTC deception claim), or (b) failure to test whether "editorial / clean beauty" style rankings systematically disadvantage talent on a protected axis even without intent — Rite Aid's core violation was *not testing for* disparate impact, not merely *having* disparate impact. Build in periodic disparate-impact testing of search rankings as documented FTC-defensive practice.

### 4.2 CCPA/CPRA — biometric information, sensitive PI, and the new ADMT rules

- **Biometric information under CPRA** is "sensitive personal information" — but the CPRA's definition requires the data be "used or intended to be used" to establish identity (narrower than pre-CPRA CCPA's "can be used" standard). A style/aesthetic embedding that is never used to identify the talent across contexts arguably falls **outside** CPRA's "biometric information" sub-definition — but it likely still qualifies as ordinary "personal information" (it's linked to an identified consumer's account) and, if the underlying photo depicts the person, may itself already be personal information regardless of the embedding's biometric status.
- **No case law or CPPA guidance directly addresses whether a CLIP-style embedding vector counts as "biometric information."** This is an open interpretive question; treat conservatively — if your embeddings are ever repurposed or become capable of cross-photo identity matching, they'd clearly cross into CPRA's sensitive-PI bucket, triggering opt-out-of-sale/sharing rights and heightened notice obligations.
- **New CPPA ADMT/risk-assessment/cybersecurity-audit regulations:** finalized and approved by California OAL on **September 23, 2025**, effective **January 1, 2026**, with the ADMT-specific opt-out/pre-use-notice/appeal obligations phasing in by **April 1, 2027** ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/california-finalizes-cppa-regulations); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/08/cppa-board-finalizes-new-rules-on-admt-cybersecurity-audits-and-risk-assessments)). ADMT is defined narrowly as tech that processes personal information and **substantially replaces human decision-making** for a "significant decision" — the regulation's enumerated significant-decision categories are finance, housing, education, **employment**, and healthcare (not advertising). Whether agency casting-shortlisting counts as "employment" for this purpose has the same unresolved employment-relationship question as §§2.3 and 3 above. If it does, Pholio (or the agency deploying the search, more precisely — CPPA rules primarily bind the "business" making the significant decision, which is more likely the agency-user than Pholio-the-platform, though platform-as-service-provider obligations also apply) would need a pre-use notice, opt-out mechanism, and human-appeal path by April 2027.
- **Recommendation:** design the search feature so the agency's human reviewer always makes the final call (never fully automates a "yes/no represent this person" decision) — this keeps you outside "substantially replaces human decision-making" under the ADMT definition regardless of how the employment question resolves, and is also your best EU AI Act and LL144 mitigant (see §7).

### 4.3 NY Fashion Workers Act (effective June 19, 2025) — digital replica consent

- Requires **separate, explicit written consent** before a model management company or client creates/uses a model's "digital replica" (AI-enhanced or computer-generated representation substantially replicating appearance/performance), specifying scope, purpose, duration, and compensation ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/01/new-york-state-enacts-fashion-workers-act); [Benesch](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/seeing-double-new-york-fashion-workers-act-creates-new-consent-requirements-for-use-of-generative-ai-tools-to-create-models-digital-replicas/)). Also voids any power-of-attorney clause purporting to grant digital-replica rights as a condition of representation (as of June 19, 2025).
- **Does descriptive photo analysis (captions, embeddings for search) trigger this?** No — the statute targets *creation of a new synthetic representation* (a generative "replica" that substitutes for the model), not descriptive analysis or retrieval indexing of the model's own real, unaltered photographs. Your design generates no new image or synthetic likeness; it only indexes existing self-submitted photos for retrieval. This should fall outside the Act's digital-replica consent trigger. **Caveat:** if you ever add a "generate a comp-card caption/description that reads like promotional copy attributed to the model" or any generative reproduction of the model's face/body, revisit this — that would implicate the Act.
- Relevant regardless: NY DOL FAQ on the Act confirms model management companies have general duties (registration, trust accounts, contract terms) independent of the digital-replica provision — not directly triggered by your feature, but worth being aware of as the broader regulatory container you sit near.

### 4.4 NY "Synthetic Performer" disclosure law (S.8420-A/A.8887-B, effective June 9, 2026)

- Requires advertisers/agencies with **actual knowledge** that an ad uses a "synthetic performer" — a digitally created/AI-generated asset intended to create the impression of a human performer who is **not recognizable as any identifiable natural performer** — to conspicuously disclose that fact in the ad ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai); [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/new-york-synthetic-performer-law-what-advertisers-need-to-know)). Penalties $1,000 first violation / $5,000 subsequent.
- **Not applicable to Pholio's core feature.** Your search returns real talent's real photographs, not a generated "synthetic performer" standing in for a person. This law is about disclosure in *outward-facing advertisements*, not internal agency search/discovery tooling. No action needed unless Pholio itself later builds ad-generation or synthetic-comp-card features.

## 5. Anti-discrimination law in casting (Title VII, NYSHRL, FEHA) and platform precedent

### 5.1 Federal / state employment discrimination framework

- **Title VII BFOQ:** race can **never** be a bona fide occupational qualification, under any circumstance — this is settled, explicit statutory text. Sex/national-origin-adjacent "authenticity" BFOQs exist narrowly in entertainment casting (e.g., casting a specific gender for a role), but courts have been skeptical even there (e.g., criticism of excluding actors from auditioning for cross-gender roles based on assumed inability to "convincingly" play them) ([UCLA Chicano Studies brief](https://www.chicano.ucla.edu/files/LPIB_14December2006_001.pdf); [Skoler Abbott](https://www.skoler-abbott.com/2016/09/28/are-casting-calls-for-actors-of-certain-races-or-national-origins-illegal/)).
- **First Amendment/artistic-expression defense** is the actual doctrinal basis studios/casting rely on for race- or ethnicity-specific casting calls for a *dramatic role*, not a BFOQ defense (which race can't satisfy) — this is why casting calls for a specific ethnicity for a specific character are common and largely unchallenged, framed as expressive casting of a creative work rather than employment discrimination.
- **This matters for Pholio because your platform is not itself doing the casting** — it's indexing talent and returning matches to agency users who make the actual casting/representation decisions. The discrimination-law exposure sits primarily with the *agency/casting client* making the ultimate selection, not with Pholio as the search-tool provider — analogous to how job boards are not typically liable for an individual employer's discriminatory hiring decision. But **platform design choices can create independent liability** if the platform itself builds/promotes discriminatory sorting (see §5.3 on intermediary liability limits).

### 5.2 NY Human Rights Law / NYC Human Rights Law — height/weight

- NYC's height/weight discrimination law (Intro. 209-A, effective **Nov 22, 2023** — older than your 2025–2026 window, flagged) added height and weight as protected classes citywide, with a narrow entertainment/expressive-casting consideration still being worked out; officials have signaled they will **not** blanket-exempt all casting calls or all modeling work, and will weigh expressive-speech claims case by case ([RPJ Law](https://rpjlaw.com/will-nycs-new-employment-discrimination-bill-restrict-casting-in-the-heights/); [Seyfarth](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/understanding-new-york-citys-height-and-weight-discrimination-law-essential-insights-for-employers.html)). Since modeling briefs routinely specify height/weight/measurement ranges (a real, non-discretionary industry practice per the `industry` skill's domain knowledge), **agencies using Pholio to filter/search on self-declared height/weight/measurements sit in a genuinely contested zone under NYC law** — not clearly protected by an expressive-casting carve-out, not clearly prohibited either. This is a pre-existing agency-side legal question independent of your AI feature (agencies already filter on these attributes manually), but a semantic search that explicitly enables filtering/ranking by body measurements makes the practice more visible/auditable, which cuts both ways (easier to defend as legitimate industry practice with documentation; also easier for a regulator/plaintiff to point to as systematic).
- **FEHA** does not list height or weight as an explicitly protected category (unlike NYC); weight-based claims in CA proceed via disability (severe obesity) or sex-stereotyping theories, not direct appearance protection.

### 5.3 Self-declared heritage filters at existing casting/modeling platforms

- **Casting Networks** explicitly markets "ethnic appearance" as a searchable/filterable attribute, alongside representation, gender, hair, eye color, framed as supporting "diverse and authentic" casting ([Casting Networks](https://www.castingnetworks.com/news/how-casting-networks-helps-you-cast-with-diversity-and-authenticity/)).
- **Backstage** takes an explicitly different approach: gender/ethnicity/age-range fields in a role description are treated as descriptive of the *character*, not hard eligibility filters — any performer can apply regardless of listed traits, and Backstage's search deliberately broadens gender-identity matching rather than narrowing it, positioning this as bias mitigation ([Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/discover/more-diverse-inclusive-casting-75330/)).
- No published enforcement action or discrimination suit against either platform specifically for offering these filters was found in this research pass — consistent with the pattern that liability, where it exists, attaches to the *casting decision-maker* using the filter for a discriminatory purpose, not to the platform for offering the field, provided the field is self-declared/first-person data (not the platform inferring the trait) and the platform doesn't itself steer or discriminate in how results surface. This structurally matches Pholio's stated design (self-declared heritage filtering only, never AI-inferred).

### 5.4 Section 230 / intermediary liability — limits, not a blanket shield

- Section 230 immunizes platforms from liability for *third-party content* but **not** for the platform's own conduct or for content/features the platform itself generates or designs (e.g., its own ranking algorithm's outputs, its own categorization logic) ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/issues/cda230); [Cato](https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/circumventing-section-230-product-liability-lawsuits-threaten-internet-speech)). Post-*Gonzalez*/*Taamneh* and adjacent litigation, courts (and Justice Sotomayor specifically, in dicta) have expressed discomfort with extending 230 immunity to cover "an algorithm that inherently discriminates against people" — i.e., if Pholio's *own* ranking/matching logic (not merely third-party listings) produces systematically discriminatory outcomes, 230 is a weaker shield than commonly assumed, because you may be treated as the "information content provider" of the ranking/categorization itself, not merely a passive host of talent-submitted content.
- **Practical implication:** the self-declared-fields design (heritage filter = talent's own stated data, passed through) is much safer under both discrimination law and 230 than any AI-inferred categorization would be, precisely because Pholio isn't generating the classification — it's relaying the talent's own statement. This reinforces your existing design choice (§ per your prompt: "heritage filtering uses only the talent's own self-declaration") as the correct one; do not let a future feature quietly slide into AI-inferred ethnicity/appearance categories, as that would both re-trigger BIPA/EU AI Act biometric-categorisation exposure (§§1, 2) *and* weaken your 230 posture.

## 6. Industry practice: what casting/model platforms actually do (2025–2026)

- **ICDA (International Casting Directors' Association) — 2026 Statement on AI in Casting** ([source](https://the-icda.com/protocols/2026/icda-statement-on-the-use-of-ai-in-casting/)): endorses AI as a **support tool only** (breakdowns, script/search assistance, self-tape triage) with the explicit principle that "AI should support, not replace, human-driven casting" and final decisions must stay human. Explicitly **prohibits**: (1) AI-generated casting suggestions that bypass casting directors, (2) "unauthorised use of biometric data or actors' likenesses," (3) training AI on CDs'/actors' proprietary data without authorization, (4) repurposing actor data submitted for casting/visibility for anything else, especially AI training. Requires clear documentation of how AI systems function and CD opt-in/opt-out control. **This is directly on-point industry-professional guidance and Pholio's design (opt-in, purpose-limited, human-in-the-loop, no repurposing for training beyond the declared search feature) tracks it closely** — worth citing internally as an industry-alignment reference.
- **AI casting-assist platforms (allcasting, Writeseen, Jotform casting agents, etc.)** increasingly market AI matching of performer profile/portfolio data against casting-call requirements — the trend is toward AI-assisted shortlisting as an accepted 2025–2026 norm, generally framed around consent and non-repurposing of data as the differentiator between acceptable and scammy tools (see also "How to Spot AI Casting Scams" — [Casting Networks](https://www.castingnetworks.com/news/how-to-spot-ai-casting-scams-protect-likeness/), flagging unauthorized AI use of a performer's likeness as the primary 2025–2026 scam vector talent are warned about).
- **Talent agencies (CAA) and SAG-AFTRA** have taken a hard public line in 2025–2026 specifically against *opt-out* consent defaults for AI use of a performer's image/likeness (criticizing Meta's "Muse" tool and Instagram photo use for AI training as opt-out rather than opt-in) — reinforcing that **opt-in is now the industry-expected consent posture** for any AI processing of talent images, not merely a nice-to-have ([Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/caa-calls-out-meta-muse-ai-video-photo-tool-instagram-1236642814/); [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/sag-aftra-slams-meta-ai-instagram-photos-opt-out-1236806350/)). Pholio's explicit-opt-in design is aligned with, and should be marketed as aligned with, this 2025–2026 industry consensus.
- No public reporting found (in this research pass) of a modeling/casting platform publishing detailed technical consent language specifically for *embedding-based semantic photo search* (as distinct from generative-AI/digital-replica consent, which is well-documented via NYFWA compliance). **Pholio would likely be an early/first mover in publishing granular, embedding-specific consent language** — an opportunity to set a defensible, publishable standard rather than a gap to be nervous about.

## 7. Design recommendations, categorized by risk tolerance

Legend: 🟢 **Clearly fine** — well-supported by the law/guidance reviewed, low litigation surface. 🟡 **Fine with named safeguards** — legally defensible but requires specific, documented mitigations to stay there. 🔴 **Do not do** — meaningfully elevated exposure under current law/guidance, or squarely against your own stated principles.

### 🟢 Clearly fine
1. **Text embeddings of self-declared bio/stats/lanes for semantic search.** No biometric-law surface (no image processing), no Article 5(1)(g)/Annex III point 1 exposure (not biometric categorization), straightforward personal-information handling under CCPA. Standard opt-in + purpose limitation is sufficient.
2. **Self-declared heritage/ethnicity filter, passed through verbatim from the talent's own statement, never AI-inferred.** Matches how Casting Networks/Backstage already operate; keeps Pholio out of the "information content provider" role for that classification (§5.4); avoids BIPA/EU-biometric-categorisation risk entirely since no biometric data is used to derive it.
3. **Caption-then-embed using an attribute-neutral, explicitly-scoped vocabulary** (shot type, styling register, mood, lighting, wardrobe, setting, expression) as your primary retrieval mechanism, with the caption model's system prompt hard-banning race/ethnicity/skin-tone/age/health/disability terms and outputs spot-audited for leakage. This is the single best structural mitigant across BIPA (moves away from "scan of face geometry" toward descriptive text), EU AI Act 5(1)(g)/Annex III pt.1 (no biometric categorization on protected axes), and FTC (documented, testable, non-deceptive purpose limitation).
4. **Human-in-the-loop for every representation/casting decision** (search surfaces candidates; a person decides). Keeps you outside CCPA's ADMT "substantially replaces human decision-making" trigger, strengthens your EU AI Act Annex III pt.4 posture even if classified as high-risk (human oversight is itself a core Annex III obligation, so you'd already be compliant on that axis), and is exactly what ICDA's 2026 statement asks of AI casting tools industry-wide.

### 🟡 Fine with named safeguards
5. **Raw whole-image CLIP/SigLIP embeddings (in addition to, or instead of, caption-then-embed) for "find talent whose photos resemble this reference image."** Legally usable, but carries the residual BIPA "scan of face geometry" risk (§1.1) that captions don't. Safeguards to attach:
   - Standalone BIPA/CUBI-grade written consent (§1.6) even outside Illinois/Texas, as your baseline global consent text.
   - Architecturally prevent any cross-photo *identity* matching capability — do not build or expose a "find this same person elsewhere" function; keep the embedding model's use strictly to style/aesthetic similarity, and document this purpose limitation in your model card/spec so you can invoke Texas TRAIGA's AI-model-training exemption (§1.3) and rebut "used to identify" theories (§1.1).
   - Retention tied strictly to discoverability (delete embeddings within a fixed SLA of opt-out/deletion — recommend ≤30 days).
   - Periodic technical audit confirming the embedding space doesn't cluster by protected attributes (skin tone, apparent age) as a proxy — if it does, that's your Article 5(1)(g)/effect-based-discrimination exposure materializing even without intent.
6. **EU-resident talent / EU-established agency users of the photo-similarity search.** Build toward Annex III point 4 (and possibly point 1) compliance now — human oversight (already recommended above), a documented risk-management process, technical documentation, and a FRIA-equivalent assessment — even though classification is contested (§2.3), because the guidance timeline (final Commission guidance expected only "by the end of 2026," obligations plausibly already live from Aug 2026) means you cannot safely wait for certainty. Cheapest path: geofence EU users into a version of the tool with mandatory-human-review framing made explicit in the UI, plus a published transparency notice (Article 50-style) stating an AI system assists the search.
7. **NYC-facing "shortlist for representation" use of the search tool.** Given DCWP's 2026 enforcement-tightening signal (§3) and the unresolved "employment agency" question, maintain LL144-bias-audit-ready documentation (methodology, periodic impact-ratio testing by sex/race/ethnicity of who gets surfaced/signed) even without a final legal determination that LL144 applies — cheap now, expensive to retrofit under an active DCWP investigation.
8. **Filtering/ranking by self-declared height/weight/measurements** (a legitimate, unavoidable industry practice) for NYC-connected agencies/talent. NYC's height/weight law (§5.2) has no clean casting carve-out yet. Safeguard: keep this as a *self-declared, talent-initiated* field (never AI-estimated from photos — which you've already excluded) and document it as reflecting long-standing, industry-necessary casting/measurement practice (per the `industry` skill's domain authority) rather than a Pholio-invented filter, to preserve the expressive-casting argument.

### 🔴 Do not do
9. **Any AI inference of race, ethnicity, skin tone, age, health/disability status, or other protected/sensitive attribute from a photograph** — even as an internal signal never surfaced to agencies. This is squarely inside EU AI Act Article 5(1)(g)'s prohibited zone if it reaches EU subjects, is the paradigmatic FTC/Rite Aid failure mode if undocumented/untested, and is the one practice you've already correctly ruled out. Do not let a future "improve search relevance" feature quietly reintroduce this via a general-purpose VLM captioner that isn't tightly prompt-constrained and audited — this is the most likely accidental-reintroduction vector.
10. **Face-geometry templates or any identity-matching/face-recognition capability**, even framed as a "dedupe accounts" or "verify it's really you" feature. This is the exact Patel/Clearview fact pattern (§1.1) and would convert your entire embedding pipeline into BIPA/CUBI-regulated territory with no ambiguity left in your favor.
11. **Treating publicly-available or third-party-sourced images (scraped comp cards, social media, agency mother-agency submissions not directly uploaded by the talent) the same as self-uploaded photos for embedding/consent purposes.** TRAIGA's and CUBI's favorable "self-published" consent theory (§1.3) and your own opt-in design both depend on the photo being the talent's own submission with their own consent — do not extend the pipeline to ingest third-party-sourced imagery without a separate, photo-source-specific consent flow.
12. **Opt-out-by-default for embedding/AI processing**, or bundling this consent into general platform ToS rather than a standalone flow. This is now an explicit industry flashpoint (CAA/SAG-AFTRA vs. Meta, §6) — an opt-out default would be read industry-wide as regressive and is also weaker under BIPA's written-consent requirement and CPRA's sensitive-PI opt-in expectations.

## Summary of what's genuinely unsettled (flag prominently to stakeholders)

1. Whether a non-identification image embedding is a BIPA "scan of face geometry" — split in IL federal courts on the adjacent "must it identify" question, no direct ruling on embeddings.
2. Whether EU AI Act Annex III point 4 (or point 1) reaches talent-representation/casting search at all, given talent aren't employees of the searching agency — no Commission guidance yet, and the guidance that exists is still in draft as of mid-2026.
3. The EU AI Act's own high-risk-obligation timeline is moving (digital omnibus simplification proposals in 2026) — don't hard-code August 2026 as the only relevant date.
4. Whether NYC LL144 reaches talent agencies at all (they aren't classic "employers" of the talent, and their relationship to "employment agency" status under overlapping NY statutes isn't harmonized) — no DCWP guidance on modeling/casting platforms.
5. Colorado's AI-law landscape changed materially in 2026 (enjoined, then replaced) — anything read before mid-2026 describing SB 24-205 as the live law is now stale.
6. Whether embedding vectors constitute CPRA "biometric information" at all — no CPPA guidance found.
